/ Sustainability & Accessibility

Who gets included, who gets erased.

Two questions running through this whole portfolio. The first asks what AI infrastructure costs. The second asks who this website was built for.

Sustainability

My research

For Make 11, I researched the water cost of AI data centers across three case studies: West Des Moines, Iowa; Mesa, Arizona; and the Amsterdam region in the Netherlands. The full visualization and reflection are on that project page. What I want to surface here is the systemic finding, not the case-by-case detail.

In Iowa, Microsoft operates five data center campuses that use up to 11.5 million gallons of water per month for cooling — roughly the equivalent of 17 Olympic swimming pools every 30 days. In Arizona, Google holds a permit equivalent to the annual water use of 23,000 Arizonans for its Mesa campus, drawing partly from a Colorado River already in long-term drought. The Netherlands offers a contrast: after sustained public pressure, the national government banned new hyperscale data center construction in 2022. Zero new facilities have been approved since.

The pattern across all three is consistent: data center water use is large, frequently opaque, and rarely weighed against the needs of the communities surrounding it.

Personal reflection

I use AI tools constantly — multiple tools, multiple times a day. A single ChatGPT query uses an estimated 4.55g of CO₂ equivalent. The water cost behind that query is harder to quantify, but this project clarified that my individual query is one of billions being processed in facilities drawing millions of gallons from communities that often had no say in those facilities being built there.

I am willing to be more intentional about which tasks genuinely need AI and which I reach for out of habit. Not every draft, search, or task requires a language model. Introducing that pause — does this actually need AI? — is the most honest commitment I can make.

I am not willing to pretend that opting out of work-related AI use is a meaningful lever. The tools are embedded in workflows and expected by employers. Saying so honestly matters more than performing a refusal I can't sustain.

Steps I took to reduce this site's environmental impact

  • Compressed images. Every image on this site is under 500KB. The largest is the infographic at 416KB. Photographic assets were resized and re-saved before being committed to the repo.
  • No third-party trackers. No analytics, no advertising scripts, no social media pixels. The only third-party assets are Google Fonts (loaded once and cached) and three embedded iframes for the Datawrapper map, the Twine game, and the speculative newspaper.
  • Static site, no server. The site is served by GitHub Pages as plain static HTML and CSS. No backend, no database, no Node process, no persistent compute.
  • Minimal JavaScript. One ~100-line script handles theme toggle, mobile menu, search, and filter. No frameworks, no build step, no large bundle.
  • System font fallbacks. If Google Fonts fails to load, the site falls back to Georgia and monospace system fonts so no critical content depends on a third-party request.
  • Lazy-loaded images. Project card images use loading="lazy" so they only load when scrolled into view.

Crawford (2021) writes that the "cloud" metaphor presents AI infrastructure as weightless and consequence-free, when infrastructure is in fact the heaviest thing in the system. The decisions above won't change that. They are small, individual choices in the face of a structural problem. But they are an attempt, in a small way, not to participate in the metaphor.

Systemic vs. individual

What can individuals do? Use AI intentionally rather than reflexively. Ask where AI infrastructure is built and who bears the environmental cost. Support transparency legislation. Pressure institutions to account for sustainability in their AI adoption decisions. Stop treating "the cloud" as weightless.

What requires systemic change? Mandatory public disclosure of water and energy use for all data centers. Community consent processes before permits are granted. Restrictions on water-intensive builds in drought-stressed regions. Regulatory frameworks with real enforcement — like the Netherlands' 2022 national ban. Accountability for indirect water costs (power-plant cooling) that companies currently exclude from their reported figures.

Who is responsible? Primarily the companies that build and operate data centers. They make the siting decisions, negotiate the water rights, and benefit from the opacity. Governments bear responsibility too — for incentivizing expansion without adequate environmental review. Individuals are responsible for not looking away, and for resisting the framing that personal consumption habits are the core of the problem. The Netherlands shows what a systemic response to a systemic problem looks like.

Accessibility

This site is committed to being usable by readers regardless of how they access it. The decisions below are specific. They were made deliberately. Some are documented in the code itself.

Semantic HTML & heading hierarchy

Every page uses a single <h1> for the page title, followed by <h2> for major sections and <h3> for subsections. Heading levels are never skipped. This matters because screen readers allow users to navigate a page by jumping between headings — if heading levels are skipped or used for visual styling rather than structure, that navigation breaks.

Semantic HTML elements (<nav>, <main>, <footer>, <article>, <aside>) are used everywhere they have a structural role.

Alt text

Every image has descriptive alt text. The alt text describes what the image shows, not just what it is. The Kumu network visualization is described as "Network graph showing app relationships clustered around Google, Apple, Meta, Microsoft." Decorative elements that convey no information use empty alt attributes so screen readers skip them.

Color contrast

All text-background color combinations were tested against the WCAG AA standard of 4.5:1 contrast ratio (3:1 for large text). Both light and dark modes meet the standard. I re-tested after every palette adjustment because contrast failures often appear when a color is changed in isolation.

Navigation & link text

The navigation bar is consistent across every page and uses descriptive link text. No "click here." No "read more" with no destination. The current page is marked with aria-current="page" so screen readers can announce it.

Responsive design

The layout is responsive and tested on desktop (1920px, 1280px) and mobile (375px) viewports. Font sizes use relative units (rem) so text scales correctly when a user changes their browser's base font size. The mobile menu collapses below 700px and uses a slide-in panel that can be dismissed by tapping outside.

Dark mode

Light and dark themes are both available via the toggle in the top right of every page. The site respects the operating system's prefers-color-scheme setting on first visit and remembers the user's choice in localStorage after that.

Reduced motion

All animations and transitions respect the prefers-reduced-motion media query. Users who have asked their operating system to minimize animation will get a site that doesn't move.

Known limitations

The embedded Datawrapper visualization (Make 11), the Kumu network map (Make 7), and the Google My Maps embed (Make 6) are third-party tools with their own accessibility implementations that I cannot fully control. Each is accompanied by a full text description on its project page so that users who cannot interact with the visual still have access to the information.
