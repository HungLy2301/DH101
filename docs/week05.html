Make 05 · Week 05 Text analysis · Comparative reading Tools: Voyant + GPT

What Voyant and GPT Can't Feel

Two machines read Sojourner Truth's 1851 speech. One counts. One interprets. Neither pauses. Neither feels the grief in the line about children sold into slavery.

The Artifact

Text analyzed: "Ain't I a Woman?" by Sojourner Truth (1851)

Voyant Cirrus: word cloud weighted by frequency.

Voyant Trends: term frequency across document segments.

Your Take

Voyant's reading

The most frequent words were rights, woman, sojourner, ain't, and women. The repetition of woman and rights confirms that the speech centers on gender and political recognition. The trends view showed that rights peaks in the middle of the text, while woman increases toward the later segments, suggesting a shift from biography to direct argument.

Voyant helped me see structural emphasis. It quantified repetition and showed how certain terms cluster in particular sections. But it treated the speech as a dataset. It could not distinguish between irony, grief, or theological reversal. It showed patterns but did not explain their force.

GPT's reading

I asked GPT for a literary analysis. It produced a rhetorical and thematic interpretation — repetition, embodiment, the intersection of race and gender, tone shifts, biblical references. It read "Ain't I a woman?" as both a logical challenge and a moral confrontation. The analysis treated the speech as a cohesive argument shaped by lived experience.

But GPT did not show how it derived those interpretations. Its authority comes from fluency rather than visible method.

How the two machines imagine meaning

Voyant imagines meaning as measurable frequency and distribution. Importance equals repetition. GPT imagines meaning as coherence and theme. Importance equals interpretive synthesis. Voyant is transparent but silent about significance. GPT is interpretive but opaque about process.

What's lost in both

Machines "read" according to their design. Voyant reads by counting and mapping. GPT reads by generating interpretation that resembles human literary analysis. What we gain from machine reading is scale and speed. Patterns become visible instantly. What we lose is situated judgment. Neither tool experiences the historical weight of a formerly enslaved woman speaking in 1851. Meaning changes depending on method — Voyant fragments the text into units; GPT reconstructs it into argument. Human reading still decides why those patterns and arguments matter.

Attribution & AI Use

Text
Sojourner Truth, "Ain't I a Woman?" 1851 speech, Akron Women's Rights Convention. Public domain.
Tools used
Voyant Tools (voyant-tools.org) for word frequency, Cirrus, and Trends analysis. ChatGPT (OpenAI) for the literary analysis comparison.
GPT prompt
"Give me a literary analysis of the text."
Voyant limitations
Cannot interpret tone, irony, or historical stakes.
GPT limitations
Does not reveal reasoning process and may overgeneralize without textual prompting.
What I changed or decided
The comparative framing — placing the two machines side by side to surface what each "imagines" meaning to be — is my own. The argument that both tools fail to feel is my own reading.

Weekly reflection · Week 05

How does AI alter authorship and remix culture? Who owns AI-made art?

AI changes authorship by making creation more collaborative and less individual. Instead of one person making everything, there are multiple layers: the person writing the prompt, the developers who built the model, and the data used to train it. Authorship becomes shared and sometimes unclear. In remix culture, AI speeds up reuse by generating new content from existing patterns. The question of ownership becomes complicated. While AI generates the output, it does not have intention or identity. I think ownership should mainly belong to the human who guides, edits, and gives meaning to the work, but being transparent about AI involvement is important.
