Make 06 · Week 06 Counter-map · Infrastructure Tool: Google My Maps

The Map They Didn't Draw

A counter-map placing Google and Meta's data centers, headquarters, and content moderation sites in the same visual field. The geography is not incidental.

Interactive map. Pan, zoom, and click pins for details. Layers: corporate HQs (red), data centers (blue), content moderation sites (orange). Open in Google My Maps →

Process Notes

Sources used

Three tiers of sources. Corporate HQs: Alphabet and Meta investor relations pages, SEC annual filings. Data centers: each company's official infrastructure pages cross-referenced with independent trackers (Baxtel.com, dgtlinfra.com) and Data Center Dynamics. Official press releases including Entergy Louisiana's December 2024 announcement supplied investment and energy data for Meta's newest campus.

Content moderation and labor: investigative journalism. Core sources: TIME's 2023 investigation of Meta's Kenya moderators, CNN Business's December 2024 reporting on PTSD diagnoses, a joint Bureau of Investigative Journalism / Foxglove April 2025 investigation of Meta's secret Ghana facility, the Washington Post's 2019 investigation of Google's Philippines operations, and Rappler / Coda Story's 2023 feature on YouTube contractors in Manila.

What was hardest to find

The content moderation layer, by a significant margin. Neither Google nor Meta publishes a list of their outsourcing vendors, the physical addresses of their moderation facilities, or contractor wages. Meta did not publicly confirm the name of its Ghana contractor for months after operations began. Google's content moderation supply chain is similarly opaque.

This structural opacity is not accidental. The outsourcing model — tech company contracts a BPO firm, which contracts local subcontractors, which hire workers — is designed to diffuse accountability. The more layers in the chain, the harder it is to assign legal responsibility for working conditions. The data missing from this map is missing precisely because its absence protects the companies that profit from the labor.

What surprised me

The geographic logic of harm concentration. Data centers cluster where energy and land are cheapest and tax abatements largest: rural Iowa, Arctic Sweden, rural Louisiana. Moderation labor clusters where wages are lowest and organized labor is weakest: Philippines, Kenya, Ghana. The pattern is not random — it follows and reinforces existing global inequalities. Google's data center in The Dalles, Oregon (population 16,000) now consumes 29% of the city's total water supply.

Your Take

This map makes one argument: AI is not virtual. It is a system of physical extraction whose costs are distributed globally while profits pool in a fifteen-mile radius of the San Francisco Peninsula.

Two headquarters coordinate a planetary infrastructure of server farms in Iowa cornfields and Arctic forests, and moderation offices in Manila and Accra that neither Google nor Meta will officially locate on any map they publish. That refusal is itself a design choice. Meta did not publicly name its Ghana contractor for nearly a year after operations began. The geography is not incidental; it follows a single optimization: push costs to wherever accountability is weakest.

If I had mapped only data centers, I would have reproduced something close to what Meta already publishes: a polished map of campuses with renewable energy statistics. My map refuses that frame. Placing moderation labor in the same visual field as physical infrastructure insists that the human cost of making AI safe be counted alongside the machine cost of running it.

D'Ignazio and Klein argue in Data Feminism that power operates through what is systematically obscured, not only through what is shown. Google and Meta's own maps are acts of curated visibility. This counter-map attempts the opposite. What remains missing are the names of individual workers, their wages, and the NDAs they signed. That data has never been collected because collecting it would force accountability. The missing data is the argument.

Weekly reflection · Week 06

What do we gain/lose when machines "read" literature for us?

When machines read literature, they change how we look at it. Instead of moving slowly through each sentence, they scan everything at once. They show repetition, patterns, and structure. This can help us notice things we might overlook. But something important is missing. Reading is not just about patterns. It is also about reaction. Sometimes a line makes us pause, remember something personal, or feel uncomfortable. A machine does not pause or feel anything. It processes information, but it does not respond to it. So we gain a clearer view of structure, but we lose the personal side of reading. Machines can organize a text, but they cannot experience it.
