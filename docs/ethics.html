/ Ethics

What it costs.

An interface is not the whole system. The questions on this page are the questions every project on this site is trying, in different forms, to ask.

Bias is not a bug

The biases of AI systems are not glitches in otherwise neutral machines. They are the distribution of the training data, expressed as output. When DALL·E produces images that consistently underrepresent darker skin tones, non-Western dress, and disability, it is not because the model "got it wrong." It is because those images were statistically underrepresented in the data the model was trained on. Crawford (2021) calls this the "registry of power" — AI systems encode the priorities of the people who built them, the data they selected, and the communities they excluded.

Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major, and Shmitchell (2021) argue, in their paper on the dangers of large language models, that fluency is itself a kind of risk. When a system produces text that sounds authoritative, users tend to trust it — even when it is wrong, biased, or simply regurgitating the most common training-data pattern. The danger is not that the AI lies. The danger is that the AI sounds so coherent we forget to ask whether it knows anything.

The labor problem

The most persistent ethical thread across this semester was labor. The tools I used — language models, image generators, content platforms — are all maintained by workers whose conditions are rarely disclosed. Sarah Roberts (2019) documents the system she calls "commercial content moderation": platforms outsource the work of reviewing traumatic material through chains of contractors long enough to make legal accountability impossible. The moderators see beheadings, child abuse, and torture so that the interface stays clean. They are paid as little as $350 to $480 per year in the Philippines while the platforms they protect generate hundreds of billions in revenue.

Mary Gray and Siddharth Suri (2019) extend this analysis to the broader "ghost work" economy: data labelers, content reviewers, transcription workers — all the human labor that makes AI systems appear to work autonomously. The on-demand structure is the point. Workers complete tasks that disappear from the interface the moment they're done. They are simultaneously the people making the AI work and the people whose work the AI is designed to replace.

My infographic on the invisible workforce (Make 9) and my counter-map of Google and Meta's infrastructure (Make 6) were both attempts to insist that this labor be visible. Roberts calls the outsourcing structure "plausible deniability." The phrase is exact, and I have come back to it again and again.

Visualization is political

D'Ignazio and Klein (2020), in Data Feminism, make a point that shaped how I built every visualization this semester: power operates through what is systematically obscured, not only through what is shown. The map a company publishes of its own infrastructure is an act of curated visibility. The decision about what to put at the center of a visualization is never neutral — it determines what relationships become visible and what remains obscured.

When I built a network of my digital ecosystem, I had to choose what to center. I chose corporations rather than myself because centering the user would have implied a kind of agency the data does not support. I am not the customer; I am the product. The choice of center node is always a political choice.

The environment

The environmental ethics of AI are inseparable from the labor ethics. Crawford's framework treats AI as fundamentally extractive: minerals mined for hardware, electricity drawn from grids that are still mostly fossil-fueled, water consumed by cooling systems in regions that often cannot spare it. My data visualization on AI water use (Make 11) found that the most consistent finding across Iowa, Arizona, and the Netherlands was not the size of the figures — it was the fact that the figures are routinely hidden. Companies sign confidentiality clauses with municipalities. State disclosure requirements do not exist until legislation forces them into being. The water is being used. The number exists. Someone is choosing not to share it.

The interface is not the whole system

The unifying ethical claim across this work is that the interface lies by omission. AI tools are designed to feel clean, frictionless, and neutral. The cleanness is the achievement. Behind it are training data with documented bias, workers with documented trauma, infrastructure with documented environmental cost, and corporate accountability structures explicitly designed to make those facts hard to locate.

My responsibility as someone who uses these tools, and as someone who hopes to build a career in fields where data and modeling matter, is not to refuse AI. It is to refuse the metaphor that frames it as consequence-free. The cloud is not weightless. Generation is not automatic. Behind every output is a chain of choices, made by people under conditions, drawing on resources that come from somewhere.

Seeing that clearly is the first ethical act. Insisting that others see it is the second.
