Reflection

Reflect 1: What counts as “making” in the age of AI? What might be gained/lost when machines participate in making? In the age of AI, “making” extends beyond traditional hands-on creation to include guiding, prompting, and collaborating with machines. Making can involve defining ideas, shaping prompts, and curating outputs, while the AI executes tasks like generating images or text. When machines participate, we gain speed, scale, and access to possibilities that humans alone might not produce. However, we may lose some control over interpretation, meaning, and originality, since AI lacks understanding or intention. Authorship becomes shared and less visible, and the creative process can feel less personal. At the same time, AI opens space for new forms of collaboration, experimentation, and exploration of ideas, highlighting both the potentials and limits of human-machine creativity.

Week 2 Reflection
Reflection

Reflect 2: Can AI help us define what it means to be human, or does it reduce us to patterns? I think AI can do both: it highlights patterns in human behavior, language, and creativity, showing common threads across experiences, but it cannot fully grasp our emotions, values, or intentions. By reflecting our habits and choices, AI can help us see aspects of ourselves more clearly. At the same time, relying on patterns risks reducing the complexity of humanity to data points, overlooking individuality, context, and depth. AI can illuminate certain truths about humans, but it cannot replace the lived, subjective experience that defines what it truly means to be human.

Week 3 Reflection
Reflection

Reflect 3: What does “authenticity” mean when identity is co-constructed with AI? When identity is co constructed with AI, authenticity becomes limited. AI builds identities by combining existing data and visual patterns rather than understanding who a person truly is. Even when humans guide the prompt, the result often reflects surface level traits instead of deeper human qualities. While AI can closely match someone’s appearance with enough detail, it struggles to represent internal values, emotions, and lived experiences. In my case, when I asked AI to show my love for nature, it only added a natural background, ignoring how that value shapes my actions and beliefs. Abstract traits like kindness or feeling fulfilled are also difficult for AI to visualize. Therefore, authenticity in AI generated identity is more about visual accuracy than truly representing the complexity of a real person.

Week 4 Reflection
Reflection

Reflect 4: Is AI a collaborator, tool, or plagiarist in storytelling? I think AI can be a collaborator and a tool, but calling it a plagiarist depends on how it is used. As a tool, AI helps generate ideas, outlines, or drafts quickly. It can suggest new directions and make the writing process faster. As a collaborator, it feels like a partner you can brainstorm with, especially when you refine prompts and build on what it gives you. However, AI does not have original experiences or emotions. It creates stories based on patterns it learned from existing texts. Because of that, there is a risk of repeating or closely echoing other works. I do not see AI itself as intentionally plagiarizing, since it does not have intent. The responsibility falls on the human using it. In storytelling, AI works best as support, while the human remains the true author and decision-maker.

Reflect 5: How does AI alter authorship and remix culture? Who owns AI-made art?

AI changes authorship by making creation more collaborative and less individual. Instead of one person making everything, there are multiple layers involved: the person writing the prompt, the developers who built the model, and the data used to train it. This makes authorship more shared and sometimes unclear.

In remix culture, AI speeds up reuse by generating new content from existing patterns. It acts like a large-scale remix tool, blending styles and structures quickly. The question of ownership becomes complicated. While AI generates the output, it does not have intention or identity. I think ownership should mainly belong to the human who guides, edits, and gives meaning to the work, but being transparent about AI involvement is important. AI shifts authorship from a single creator to a collaborative process shaped by both human choice and machine pattern.

Week 6 Reflection
Reflection

Reflect 6: What do we gain/lose when machines “read” literature for us? When machines read literature, they change how we look at it. Instead of moving slowly through each sentence, they scan everything at once. They show repetition, patterns, and structure. This can help us notice things we might overlook, like how often a certain word appears or where an idea becomes stronger in the text. But something important is missing. Reading is not just about patterns. It is also about reaction. Sometimes a line makes us pause, remember something personal, or feel uncomfortable. A machine does not pause or feel anything. It processes information, but it does not respond to it. So we gain a clearer view of structure, but we lose the personal side of reading. Machines can organize a text, but they cannot experience it. Meaning still depends on how a human reader connects to the words.

Reflect 7: How does AI reshape global geographies of power, labor, and environment?

AI reshapes global geographies of power, labor, and environment by separating where decisions are made from where the work and environmental costs occur. Major AI companies such as Google and Meta are headquartered in Silicon Valley, concentrating technological and economic power in a small number of places.

At the same time, much of the labor that supports AI, including content moderation and data labeling, is outsourced to workers in countries like the Philippines, Kenya, and India. AI infrastructure also requires large data centers that consume significant amounts of land, energy, and water, often located where resources are cheaper. As a result, the benefits of AI are concentrated in a few tech hubs while many of the labor and environmental impacts are distributed across other parts of the world.

What struck me most was not the individual apps but the data type nodes. My location, my AI prompts, my financial behavior, my social graph — these sit at the center of the network as bridges connecting platforms that seem completely unrelated to each other.

Building my personal AI ecosystem network made the invisible suddenly visible. On the surface, I interact with dozens of apps daily (Spotify, Gmail, TikTok, ChatGPT) but the network revealed that nearly everything flows back to four corporations: Google, Apple, Meta, and Microsoft. My location, my AI prompts, my financial behavior, my social graph — these sit at the center of the network as bridges connecting platforms that seem completely unrelated to each other. What the visualization obscures is just as important. I have no node. The person generating all this data is absent from the map, which itself is a statement about power. I am not a participant in this network, I am its raw material. Also invisible are the data brokers, the advertisers who are the actual customers, and the workers and communities whose labor and resources make the physical infrastructure possible.

Reflect 9: What is creativity when AI can generate endlessly? Where is the human in generative work? Creativity used to mean making something from nothing. Generative AI complicates that because the making happens automatically and without intention. What is left for the human is not creation in the traditional sense but selection and taste. Building the Tracery bot made this clear quickly. I was in control of the rules and vocabulary, but not the output. The tool combined my words in ways I did not plan. Some results were flat and forgettable. Others felt more resonant than anything I would have written on purpose.

Reflect 9: What is creativity when AI can generate endlessly? Where is the human in generative work?

Creativity used to mean making something from nothing. Generative AI complicates that because the making happens automatically and without intention. What is left for the human is not creation in the traditional sense but selection and taste. Building the Tracery bot made this clear quickly. I was in control of the rules and vocabulary, but not the output. The tool combined my words in ways I did not plan. Some results were flat and forgettable. Others felt more resonant than anything I would have written on purpose. The human in generative work lives in the margins. I wrote every word in the grammar, but the machine arranged them. I generated 60 outputs and chose 7. Those choices were mine and they reflected something real about my judgment. So while I was not in control of the output, I was in control of what it meant.

Week 10 Reflection
Reflection

Reflect 10: How does AI change our relationship to play, rules, and narrative? Building an interactive AI narrative made me realize AI doesn't just enter games as a character. It quietly rewrites what the rules mean. When an AI evaluates your choices, it isn't judging the way a human would. It's sorting you. That shift changes how narrative feels: stakes become statistical, authenticity becomes a variable. What I found most unsettling wasn't designing the AI antagonist; it was how naturally game logic and AI logic already resemble each other. Both systems reward the right outputs. Play used to feel like a space apart from that pressure. Working through this project made me wonder if it still is.

Reflect 11: Who does the invisible work of AI, and who profits from it?

The invisible work of AI is done by groups that are often overlooked. This includes data center workers who maintain the infrastructure, content moderators who review harmful material, and gig workers who label training data. There are also communities living near data centers and mining areas who face environmental impacts like water use and pollution without having much say in these decisions.

At the same time, the profits mainly go to large companies, shareholders, and high skill workers who develop and sell AI systems. There is a clear gap between those who carry the costs and those who benefit from it. Understanding AI should not only focus on technology, but also on labor and environmental impact, especially on the people who are often left invisible.

Reflect 12: Is AI sustainable? What ecological trade-offs are we willing to accept?

In its current form, AI is not sustainable. It requires large amounts of water, energy, land, and minerals, and these demands are growing quickly. Data centers are being built in areas that already face water shortages, and the environmental cost is often placed on communities that do not benefit from the technology. AI could become more sustainable, but this would require better decisions about where and how systems are built, more transparency about resource use, and limits on expansion in sensitive areas. However, current incentives focus more on growth and competition than sustainability. The real issue is not only what trade offs we accept, but who gets to decide them. Right now, the people most affected by environmental costs often have the least influence, which raises important concerns about fairness and responsibility.

Week 13 Reflection
Reflection

Reflect 13: Do we imagine AI futures as utopian, dystopian, or something in between? What do those visions reveal about us? I tend to imagine AI futures as in between, and I think that's harder to sit with than either extreme. Pure dystopia lets you off the hook; pure utopia does the same in reverse. What unsettles me more is a future where the headline numbers are good and the cost is buried in a terms of service clause nobody read. Working on this project clarified something: the futures we imagine reveal what we have already accepted. The believable 2047 I built was just 2025, extended forward until it became impossible to ignore.
