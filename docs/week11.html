Make 11 · Week 11 Data viz · Ecology Tool: Datawrapper

What the Cloud Drinks

Three case studies on the water cost of AI data centers — Iowa, Arizona, the Netherlands. The clearest finding was not about water. It was about what gets disclosed and what doesn't.

Process Notes

Sources

The research drew primarily from investigative journalism and policy reporting published between 2021 and 2026. Core sources: the Business Record's November 2025 coverage of Iowa data center water use; two Axios Des Moines pieces on the 2025 West Des Moines water crisis and the 2026 disclosure legislation; Grist's March 2026 investigation of Arizona's data center expansion; a joint Guardian/Source Material analysis of Big Tech water use in drought regions; Data Center Dynamics' coverage of Amsterdam's moratorium. For theoretical framing: Kate Crawford's Atlas of AI.

The hardest data to find

Facility-level water use figures. Tech companies consistently report water use in global aggregates rather than by individual site, which makes it nearly impossible to say with precision how much water a specific campus uses. The Microsoft Goodyear, Arizona figure (56 million gallons annually) only emerged because a journalist found it buried in a city council analysis Microsoft shared locally. The Iowa figures were available because West Des Moines Water Works is a public utility with some disclosure obligations. The Netherlands data centers, by contrast, are subject to municipal agreements that include confidentiality clauses.

The undercount limitation

The most significant uncertainty: indirect water use. The water consumed by the power plants that supply electricity to data centers is often excluded from the companies' reported figures. Grist's 2026 Arizona reporting cites projections that power-plant water use in Arizona could quadruple to meet data center demand — a figure that dwarfs the on-site usage typically reported. Any visualization that excludes indirect cost is, by definition, an undercount. This map represents direct use only, and should be read with that limitation in mind.

Your Take

The smallest number is the loudest

The environmental cost that concerns me most from this research is not the largest number I found. It is the smallest one: the confidentiality clause. In Eagle Mountain, Utah, Meta signed an agreement with the city in 2018 to keep its monthly water use figures private. In Goodyear, Arizona, Microsoft's water records were classified as proprietary until a journalist found a local council document. In Iowa, a state law requiring data centers to disclose their water use publicly did not exist until 2026. The water is being used. The number exists. Someone is choosing not to share it.

What this project is really about

That pattern — the active concealment of the resource cost of AI infrastructure — is what this project is really about. The map I built shows three locations. The actual scope of the problem is much larger and much harder to see, partly because it is designed to be.

Can AI be sustainable?

Honest answer: not in its current form, and not at its current rate of expansion. Sustainability would require siting decisions made with genuine community input, mandatory disclosure of water and energy use, regulatory limits on building in water-stressed regions, and a willingness by companies to accept constraints on growth. The Netherlands demonstrated that this is politically possible. The United States has largely demonstrated that it is politically unlikely without sustained pressure.

My responsibility

My responsibility, as I understand it, is to see the infrastructure clearly — not to accept the metaphor of the cloud as neutral, weightless, consequence-free. This project is an attempt at that clarity. What I do with it next is the harder question.

What I changed or decided
All three case studies were selected by me based on what the sources could actually support with specific, verifiable numbers — AI did not choose them. The central argument (that concealment is the subject of the project, not the water figures themselves) is entirely my own framing. The acknowledgment that the map is an undercount because it excludes indirect power-plant water use was my own editorial decision. Every factual claim — 11.5 million gallons per month in Iowa, the 23,000-resident water equivalent in Arizona, the 2018 Eagle Mountain confidentiality agreement — was verified against the primary source before inclusion.

Weekly reflection · Week 11

Who does the invisible work of AI, and who profits from it?

The invisible work of AI is done by groups that are often overlooked. This includes data center workers who maintain the infrastructure, content moderators who review harmful material, and gig workers who label training data. There are also communities living near data centers and mining areas who face environmental impacts like water use and pollution without having much say in these decisions.

At the same time, the profits mainly go to large companies, shareholders, and high-skill workers who develop and sell AI systems. There is a clear gap between those who carry the costs and those who benefit from it. Understanding AI should not only focus on technology, but also on labor and environmental impact, especially on the people who are often left invisible.
