Make 12 · Week 12 Speculative fiction · Newspaper Dateline: March 14, 2047

The Last Human Therapist

A speculative newspaper from 2047 about the retirement of the last human psychotherapist — and the law that made universal AI therapy free, with one clause buried inside.

Open the artifact in full screen →

The Setup

The artifact is a newspaper profile published March 14, 2047, in a publication called The American Register. It depicts a near-future America in which human psychotherapy has been entirely replaced by an AI platform called ARIA, operated by a fictional company called MindBridge Corp. The article is occasioned by the retirement of Dr. Miriam Osei — framed by the publication as a milestone, an "end of an era" to be noted and celebrated.

What's extrapolated, not invented

The key events leading to this future are not invented from scratch: they are extrapolations from trends already present in 2025. Insurance companies have steadily reduced reimbursement for therapy. AI mental health apps (Woebot, Wysa, and others) are already in widespread use. Telehealth has normalized remote care. Mental health provider shortages are severe and documented. The leap to 2047 imagines a federal coverage act that accelerated AI therapy's dominance by making it free and universally accessible — an undeniably appealing policy outcome.

Where the hidden cost surfaces

Through two details embedded in the reporting: Section 14(c) of the fictional CareAccess Act, which allows "commercial wellness applications" to license aggregated session data; and the advertisement at the bottom of the page, which reads "Always listening. Always learning. Always here — powered by a decade of real human insight."

The phrase "real human insight" is doing the work: it acknowledges, in the language of a marketing slogan, that the system is built on the disclosed intimacies of millions of patients who may not have understood that their grief was becoming a product.

What this future assumes

That access is the primary measure of a healthcare system's success; that anonymization is equivalent to privacy; and that the terms of a therapeutic relationship can be governed by a terms-of-service agreement without meaningful consequence.

Your Take

Not desirable, but not a fantasy

This future is not desirable, but it is not a fantasy either, which is precisely the point. Every element of it has a precedent in 2025. Insurance companies already deprioritize mental health reimbursement. AI therapy tools already collect session data. Legislation governing data use in healthcare is already riddled with provisions that most patients never read. The fictional CareAccess Act is not a dystopian invention — it is a plausible next legislative step.

Genuinely good, in measurable ways

What makes this future worth examining is that it is also genuinely good, in measurable ways. Suicide rates are down. Access is universal. Wait times are minutes, not weeks. The article does not let the reader dismiss ARIA as a failure, because it isn't one, by most available metrics. The discomfort the artifact is designed to produce is not "AI is bad" but something more specific: what happens when the frame we use to evaluate a system excludes the question of who owns the data produced by that system?

What would need to change

Strong data sovereignty law specifically covering therapeutic disclosure. Law that treats what you say to a mental health system as belonging to you, unconditionally, regardless of anonymization. Meaningful informed consent, not a terms-of-service checkbox. And a political culture willing to treat emotional data as categorically different from behavioral data — more like a confession than a transaction. Whether that culture is available to us is the question this artifact is really asking.

Attribution & AI Use

AI collaborator
Claude (Anthropic), used throughout creative development.
What I asked AI to help with
Brainstorming: I described my thematic interests (AI in healthcare, utopian-but-costly tone, 2047 dateline) and Claude generated a list of possible news hooks. I selected "profile of the last human therapist retiring" from several options it proposed.

Structural feedback: After drafting the article's outline myself, I asked Claude where the hidden cost (data ownership) could surface most naturally within journalistic conventions. It suggested embedding it as reported fact rather than editorializing — I agreed and wrote the Section 14(c) detail accordingly.

Fact-checking plausibility: I asked Claude whether the legal framing of "therapeutic data stewardship" and anonymization-as-privacy had real precedents. It pointed me toward existing HIPAA limitations and app-based therapy data practices, which I used to ground the fictional legislation in actual 2025 policy gaps.
What I did NOT use Claude for
Claude did not write the article, Dr. Osei's dialogue, the advertisement copy, or any written deliverables. All prose was written by me and revised through my own editorial judgment. The advertisement line — "Always listening. Always learning. Always here — powered by a decade of real human insight" — is original copy written by me as part of the artifact's critical argument.
Design & code
HTML and CSS written mainly by Claude with my prompts and due diligence after each output. Layout modeled on broadsheet newspaper conventions (column rules, nameplate structure, dropcap, pull quote) — referenced visually from The New York Times and The Guardian print editions, not copied from any source. Fonts: Libre Baskerville, Barlow Condensed, Barlow — all from Google Fonts under the Open Font License.
Image sources
No external images used. All visual elements are typographic and CSS-based.
Fictional sources & characters
All named individuals, organizations, legislation, and statistics within the article are fictional and invented for speculative purposes: Dr. Miriam Osei (fictional character) · MindBridge Corp. / ARIA platform (fictional company and product) · CareAccess Act of 2039 / Section 14(c) (fictional legislation) · Thomas Achebe (fictional journalist) · all statistics (287M users, 34% suicide rate decline, etc.) invented for internal narrative consistency, not drawn from real data.

Weekly reflection · Week 12 + 13

Is AI sustainable? Do we imagine AI futures as utopian, dystopian, or something in between?

In its current form, AI is not sustainable. It requires large amounts of water, energy, land, and minerals, and these demands are growing quickly. Data centers are being built in areas that already face water shortages, and the environmental cost is often placed on communities that do not benefit from the technology. AI could become more sustainable, but this would require better decisions about where and how systems are built, more transparency about resource use, and limits on expansion in sensitive areas. The real issue is not only what trade-offs we accept, but who gets to decide them. Right now, the people most affected by environmental costs often have the least influence.

I tend to imagine AI futures as in between, and I think that's harder to sit with than either extreme. Pure dystopia lets you off the hook; pure utopia does the same in reverse. What unsettles me more is a future where the headline numbers are good and the cost is buried in a terms of service clause nobody read. Working on this project clarified something: the futures we imagine reveal what we have already accepted. The believable 2047 I built was just 2025, extended forward until it became impossible to ignore.
