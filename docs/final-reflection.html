What the Machine Doesn't Show You

A reflection on making, power, and accountability in the age of AI.

I came into this semester thinking about AI the way most people do — as a tool. Something you prompt, something that outputs, something useful or occasionally frustrating. I left thinking about it as a system: a set of relationships between labor, infrastructure, capital, and meaning that is designed, at nearly every level, to make itself hard to see.

That shift did not happen in a single moment. It accumulated — through a comic, a GIF, a game, a map, a network diagram, a speculative newspaper from 2047 — and each piece cracked the same assumption from a different angle.

The first crack: who is making what

The first crack was small. When I built a comic by describing a concept to an AI image generator and watching it execute, I noticed something in the gap between what I intended and what the machine produced. The AI delivered a villain, a dramatic confrontation, an instant redemption — not because those were the best choices for the story, but because those are the structures most densely represented in its training data. It was producing formula, not meaning. I was the one who had to decide what the formula was for. That division — machine executes, human defines — felt at first like a clean arrangement. It became, over the course of the semester, considerably more complicated.

When I ran Sojourner Truth's 1851 speech through two different reading tools, I encountered the same division at a different scale. One tool counted and mapped word frequency; it could show that rights peaks in the middle of the text and woman rises toward the end, but it could not feel the grief in the line about children sold into slavery. The other tool generated fluent literary interpretation, organized themes, articulated connections — and performed understanding without demonstrating it. Neither tool experienced the historical weight of a formerly enslaved woman demanding recognition from a room that doubted her humanity. I realized that machines read according to their design: they can reveal structure at speed and scale, but human reading is still what decides why the structure matters.

From aesthetic question to political one

What changed over the semester was my understanding of what that human responsibility actually requires. Early on, I framed it as an aesthetic question — humans set the limits, humans interpret the output, humans give the work meaning. That framing is true but insufficient. Kate Crawford, in Atlas of AI, argues that AI systems are not neutral intelligences but a "registry of power" — they encode the priorities of the people who built them, the data they selected, and the communities they excluded or extracted from (Crawford, 2021). The interface hides this. Crawford's term for the dominant metaphor is the "cloud": it presents infrastructure as weightless and consequence-free, when infrastructure is, in fact, the heaviest thing in the system.

I spent the second half of the semester trying to map what the cloud conceals. When I built a network of my own digital ecosystem — every app, every platform, every data type flowing between companies — I discovered that I had no node. My location, my prompts, my professional history, my social graph all appeared in the network as abstract data types, not as a person with agency. The companies were visible; the advertisers who purchased profiles built from my behavior were not. The choice of center node in any network, I came to understand, is always a political choice. D'Ignazio and Klein argue that power operates through what is systematically obscured, not only through what is shown (D'Ignazio & Klein, 2020). The map Google publishes of its own infrastructure is an act of curated visibility. Every counter-map is an argument about what the official version leaves out.

The workers and the water

The workers were left out. When I researched the content moderation systems that keep AI-mediated platforms functional, I found a labor arrangement that Sarah Roberts, in Behind the Screen, describes with precision: "plausible deniability." Platforms outsource traumatic reviewing work through chains of contractors long enough to diffuse legal accountability (Roberts, 2019). The workers — in the Philippines, in Kenya, in Ghana — view beheadings and child abuse so that the interface stays clean and the company stays insulated. Mary Gray and Siddharth Suri, in Ghost Work, describe the broader pattern: on-demand labor that is completed just-in-time and then disappears from the interface, invisible to the user, unaccountable to the platform (Gray & Suri, 2019). This is not an oversight in AI systems. It is structural. The invisibility is load-bearing.

When I built a game that put the player in front of an AI interviewer, I tried to make that structural condition felt. The game tells you, midway through, that your hesitations will be used to train the next version of the system. You are not just being evaluated — you are being used. The measured pauses logged in decimal seconds, the percentile rankings, the data retention clause: these are the procedural argument. What the game is really saying is that the AI interview is not a conversation. It is an extraction — the same extraction, at smaller scale, that content moderators perform when they label traumatic content that simultaneously reviews it and trains the AI being built to replace them.

The future is already here

The speculative piece I wrote at the end of the semester imagined a 2047 in which AI therapy has replaced human psychotherapy — and where the emotional data disclosed in those sessions is licensed, under Section 14(c) of a fictional federal act, for commercial use. The ad at the bottom of the fictional newspaper page reads: "Always listening. Always learning. Always here — powered by a decade of real human insight." The phrase "real human insight" is doing the work: it acknowledges, in the language of a marketing slogan, that the system is built on the disclosed intimacies of patients who may not have understood that their grief was becoming a product.

Refusing the cloud metaphor

What I think it means to be human in the age of AI is to refuse the cloud metaphor — not to reject AI, but to insist on seeing it whole. The interface is designed to make generation feel clean, effortless, consequence-free. The semester was an exercise in looking behind the interface: at the training data it laundered into neutral outputs, at the workers it made invisible, at the water it drank from communities that had no say, at the emotional data it is already positioned to commodify. The machine can automate at scale, generate at speed, and optimize relentlessly. It cannot hold itself accountable. That remains, entirely, a human responsibility — and one that requires, first, being willing to see what the system was designed to hide.
