AI is not a tool. It is a system of power.

I came into this semester thinking about AI the way most people do — as a tool. Something you prompt, something that outputs, something useful or occasionally frustrating. I left thinking about it as a system: a set of relationships between labor, infrastructure, capital, and meaning that is designed, at nearly every level, to make itself hard to see.

That shift happened across twelve projects — a comic, a GIF, a Twine game, a counter-map, a network diagram, an infographic, a data visualization, a speculative newspaper from 2047 — and each one cracked the same assumption from a different angle. Who actually does the work behind AI? Where does the water go? When the machine generates an image, moderates a video, or fills a progress bar, who decided what that means — and who bears the cost of getting there?

What I've come to argue is that AI shifts authorship in ways that are real but uneven. The machine can animate, generate, moderate, and sort at a scale no human can match. But humans still define what the output signifies, set the limits on what the system can do, and — most importantly — bear the consequences when those limits fail or those costs are hidden. Being human in the age of AI is not about resisting automation. It is about refusing to look away from what automation costs.

This portfolio is a record of that shift. Each project below includes the artifact, a reflection, and a full account of how AI was and wasn't used in making it.
