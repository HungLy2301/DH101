## Process Notes
My research drew primarily from investigative journalism and policy reporting published between 2021 and 2026. The core sources were the Business Record's November 2025 coverage of Iowa data center water use, two Axios Des Moines pieces covering the 2025 West Des Moines water crisis and the 2026 disclosure legislation, Grist's March 2026 investigation into Arizona's data center expansion, a joint Guardian/Source Material analysis of Big Tech water use in drought regions, and Data Center Dynamics' coverage of Amsterdam's moratorium and its aftermath. For theoretical framing, I drew on Kate Crawford's Atlas of AI, which provides the conceptual vocabulary for understanding AI infrastructure as extractive rather than neutral.
The hardest data to find was facility-level water use figures. Tech companies consistently report water usage in global aggregates rather than by individual site, which makes it nearly impossible to say with precision how much water a specific campus uses. The Microsoft Goodyear, Arizona figure (56 million gallons annually) only emerged because a journalist found it buried in a city council analysis that Microsoft shared locally. The Iowa figures were available because West Des Moines Water Works is a public utility with some disclosure obligations. The Netherlands data centers, by contrast, are subject to municipal agreements that include confidentiality clauses.
The most significant uncertainty in this project is the question of indirect water use: the water consumed by the power plants that supply electricity to data centers is often excluded from the companies' reported figures. Grist's 2026 Arizona reporting cites projections that power-plant water use in Arizona could quadruple to meet data center demand, a figure that dwarfs the on-site usage typically reported. Any visualization of data center water impact that excludes this indirect cost is, by definition, an undercount. This project's map represents direct use only, and should be read with that limitation in mind.

## Reflection
The environmental cost that concerns me most from this research is not the largest number I found. It is the smallest one: the confidentiality clause. In Eagle Mountain, Utah, Meta signed an agreement with the city in 2018 to keep its monthly water use figures private. In Goodyear, Arizona, Microsoft's water records were classified as proprietary until a journalist found a local council document. In Iowa, a state law requiring data centers to disclose their water use publicly did not exist until 2026. The water is being used. The number exists. Someone is choosing not to share it.
That pattern of the active concealment of the resource cost of AI infrastructure is what this project is really about. The map I built shows three locations. The actual scope of the problem is much larger and much harder to see, partly because it is designed to be.
Can AI be sustainable? I think the honest answer is: not in its current form, and not at its current rate of expansion. Sustainability would require siting decisions made with genuine community input, mandatory disclosure of water and energy use, regulatory limits on building in water-stressed regions, and a willingness by companies to accept constraints on growth. The Netherlands demonstrated that this is politically possible. The United States has largely demonstrated that it is politically unlikely without sustained pressure.
My responsibility, as I understand it, is to see the infrastructure clearly, not to accept the metaphor of the cloud as neutral, weightless, consequence-free. This project is an attempt at that clarity. What I do with it next is the harder question.
