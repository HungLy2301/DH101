Final Reflection Essay (~750 words)
What the Machine Doesn't Show You
A Final Reflection on Making, Power, and Accountability in the Age of AI
I started this semester with a straightforward assumption: AI is a tool, and the interesting question is how to use it well. By the end, I had made four projects that collectively dismantled that assumption. The interesting question, it turns out, is not how to use AI — it is how to see it clearly.

The first project that cracked the assumption was my reverse-engineering of DALL·E. I had chosen it because image generation felt like the purest example of AI as creative tool — you type words, pictures appear, creativity is democratized. But the more I mapped the system — data, training, interface, labor — the harder it became to call that creativity. Kate Crawford, in Atlas of AI, argues that AI systems are not autonomous intelligences but "a registry of power" — they encode the priorities of the people who built them, the data they selected, and the communities they excluded. DALL·E's outputs look neutral. The interface makes generation feel playful and frictionless. But the training data reflects what was already on the internet: predominantly Western, predominantly English, systematically underrepresenting communities whose images were never digitized or were locked behind paywalls. The tool didn't just generate images — it reproduced a particular version of the world and presented it as universal.

That gap between what the interface shows and what the system contains became the thread I followed for the rest of the semester.

Make 4, the GIF, was where I first articulated what I now think is the central question of human authorship in the age of AI. I built a looping progress bar — filling, reaching 100%, resetting — and the assignment asked where human choice lived in an AI-assisted make. My answer then was that the machine executed the animation while I defined what the loop meant. That still feels true. But what I understand now that I didn't then is that the definition of meaning is not just an aesthetic act — it is a political one. Mary Gray and Siddharth Suri, in Ghost Work, describe the structural condition of AI labor as "on-demand work that is completed just-in-time and then disappears from the interface." The progress bar filling and resetting is, I now realize, an almost literal image of that condition: work that arrives, completes, and vanishes, leaving no trace and accumulating nothing for the worker who performed it. I wasn't just making a GIF about futility in the abstract. I was, without knowing it, making a GIF about the shape of invisible labor.

Make 10 made that connection explicit. My infographic on content moderation drew heavily on Sarah Roberts' Behind the Screen, in which she introduces the concept of "commercial content moderation" and documents what she calls the industry's "plausible deniability" — the structural arrangement by which platforms outsource the most traumatic work to contractors in the Global South, maintaining enough distance to disclaim responsibility for working conditions while benefiting entirely from the labor. What struck me most was Roberts' observation that this arrangement is not incidental to AI — it is load-bearing. Content moderation is what keeps AI-mediated platforms functional and legally defensible. The moderation is invisible by design. The platforms want it that way.

Make 11 extended the same logic from labor to infrastructure. Crawford describes AI as a system requiring "continuous extraction — of minerals, energy, water, and human labor." My research into data center water use — Microsoft in Iowa, Google in Arizona, the contrast case of the Netherlands — confirmed this in the most concrete terms I'd encountered all semester. What I found was not just that data centers use a lot of water. It was that the water use figures are frequently classified, buried in local council documents, or excluded from company sustainability reports entirely. The confidentiality clause in Meta's Eagle Mountain agreement, signed in 2018, is not an oversight — it is a decision. Someone chose not to share that number.

Across all four projects, the pattern is consistent: the most consequential aspects of AI systems — whose labor sustains them, what resources they consume, whose communities bear the cost — are the aspects least visible in the interface. The interface is designed to make AI feel clean, frictionless, and consequence-free. Crawford calls this the "cloud" metaphor: it presents infrastructure as weightless, when infrastructure is, in fact, the heaviest thing in the system.

What I think it means to be human in the age of AI is to refuse that metaphor. Not to reject AI, but to insist on seeing it whole — the data it was trained on, the workers who sustain it, the water it drinks, the agreements that keep those numbers private. The machine can automate at scale. It cannot hold itself accountable. That is still, entirely, a human job.

What the Machine Doesn't Show You
A Final Reflection on Making, Power, and Accountability in the Age of AI
I came into this semester thinking about AI the way most people do — as a tool. Something you prompt, something that outputs, something useful or occasionally frustrating. I left thinking about it as a system: a set of relationships between labor, infrastructure, capital, and meaning that is designed, at nearly every level, to make itself hard to see.

That shift did not happen in a single moment. It accumulated — through a comic, a GIF, a game, a map, a network diagram, a speculative newspaper from 2047 — and each piece cracked the same assumption from a different angle.

The first crack was small. When I built a comic by describing a concept to an AI image generator and watching it execute, I noticed something in the gap between what I intended and what the machine produced. The AI delivered a villain, a dramatic confrontation, an instant redemption — not because those were the best choices for the story, but because those are the structures most densely represented in its training data. It was producing formula, not meaning. I was the one who had to decide what the formula was for. That division — machine executes, human defines — felt at first like a clean arrangement. It became, over the course of the semester, considerably more complicated.

When I ran Sojourner Truth's 1851 speech through two different reading tools, I encountered the same division at a different scale. One tool counted and mapped word frequency; it could show that rights peaks in the middle of the text and woman rises toward the end, but it could not feel the grief in the line about children sold into slavery. The other tool generated fluent literary interpretation, organized themes, articulated connections — and performed understanding without demonstrating it. Neither tool experienced the historical weight of a formerly enslaved woman demanding recognition from a room that doubted her humanity. I realized that machines read according to their design: they can reveal structure at speed and scale, but human reading is still what decides why the structure matters.

What changed over the semester was my understanding of what that human responsibility actually requires. Early on, I framed it as an aesthetic question — humans set the limits, humans interpret the output, humans give the work meaning. That framing is true but insufficient. Kate Crawford, in Atlas of AI, argues that AI systems are not neutral intelligences but a "registry of power" — they encode the priorities of the people who built them, the data they selected, and the communities they excluded or extracted from. The interface hides this. Crawford's term for the dominant metaphor is the "cloud": it presents infrastructure as weightless and consequence-free, when infrastructure is, in fact, the heaviest thing in the system.

I spent the second half of the semester trying to map what the cloud conceals. When I built a network of my own digital ecosystem — every app, every platform, every data type flowing between companies — I discovered that I had no node. My location, my prompts, my professional history, my social graph all appeared in the network as abstract data types, not as a person with agency. The companies were visible; the advertisers who purchased profiles built from my behavior were not. The choice of center node in any network, I came to understand, is always a political choice. D'Ignazio and Klein argue that power operates through what is systematically obscured, not only through what is shown. The map that Google publishes of its own infrastructure is an act of curated visibility. Every counter-map is an argument about what the official version leaves out.

The workers were left out. When I researched the content moderation systems that keep AI-mediated platforms functional, I found a labor arrangement that Sarah Roberts, in Behind the Screen, describes with precision: "plausible deniability." Platforms outsource traumatic reviewing work through chains of contractors long enough to diffuse legal accountability. The workers — in the Philippines, in Kenya, in Ghana — view beheadings and child abuse so that the interface stays clean and the company stays insulated. Mary Gray and Siddharth Suri, in Ghost Work, describe the broader pattern: on-demand labor that is completed just-in-time and then disappears from the interface, invisible to the user, unaccountable to the platform. This is not an oversight in AI systems. It is structural. The invisibility is load-bearing.

When I built a game that put the player in front of an AI interviewer, I tried to make that structural condition felt. The game tells you, midway through, that your hesitations will be used to train the next version of the system. You are not just being evaluated — you are being used. The measured pauses logged in decimal seconds, the percentile rankings, the data retention clause: these are the procedural argument. What the game is really saying is that the AI interview is not a conversation. It is an extraction — the same extraction, at smaller scale, that content moderators perform when they label traumatic content that simultaneously reviews it and trains the AI being built to replace them.

3. Accessibility Page — Full Content
Accessibility
This page documents the specific accessibility decisions I made while building this site, and why I made them.

Semantic HTML and Heading Structure
Every page on this site uses a single <h1> for the page title, followed by <h2> for major sections and <h3> for subsections. I never skip heading levels. This matters because screen readers allow users to navigate a page by jumping between headings — if heading levels are skipped or used for visual styling rather than structure, that navigation breaks.

I also used semantic HTML elements (<nav>, <main>, <footer>, <article>, <section>) rather than generic <div> tags wherever the element had a structural role. This gives assistive technologies meaningful information about what each region of the page is for.

Alt Text
Every image on this site has descriptive alt text. I wrote alt text that describes what the image shows, not just what it is. For example:

The network visualization is described as: "Network graph showing relationships between 30 nodes representing apps, companies, and data types in a personal digital ecosystem. Central nodes include Google, Apple, Meta, and Microsoft."
The comic panels are described individually, noting characters, action, and dialogue for users who cannot see the images.
Decorative elements that convey no information use empty alt attributes (alt="") so screen readers skip them.
Color Contrast
I checked all text-background color combinations using the WebAIM Contrast Checker. All body text meets the WCAG AA standard of 4.5:1 contrast ratio minimum. Headings, which are larger, meet the 3:1 ratio required for large text. I specifically re-tested after adding any new color to the design, because contrast failures often appear when a palette is adjusted.

Navigation
The navigation bar is consistent across every page and uses descriptive link text. I replaced all vague link text ("click here," "read more") with text that describes the destination ("View the full network on Kumu," "Read the final reflection"). This matters for screen reader users, who often navigate by pulling up a list of all links on a page — vague link text becomes meaningless out of context.

The site logo/name in the top left returns to the homepage on every page, which is a standard convention that sighted and non-sighted users both rely on.

Responsive Design
The layout is responsive and tested on both desktop and mobile viewports. Font sizes use relative units (rem) rather than fixed pixels, which means the text scales correctly when a user changes their browser's base font size — a common accessibility accommodation.

Known Limitations and What I Would Fix Next
The embedded Datawrapper visualization and the Kumu network map are third-party tools with their own accessibility implementations that I cannot fully control. Both are accompanied by full text descriptions on their respective pages so that users who cannot interact with the visual still have access to the information. If I rebuilt this site with more time, I would either find accessible alternatives or build the visualizations natively so I could control their markup directly.
